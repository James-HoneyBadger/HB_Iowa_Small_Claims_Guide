## Learning objectives

Define pro se; identify the responsibilities of self-representation; distinguish court information from legal advice; recognize limits on representing others; identify official assistance and when to consult counsel.

## What pro se means

Pro se means appearing on one's own behalf without an attorney. Iowa Judicial Branch guidance expressly states that a lawyer is optional in small claims, and its glossary notes that self-representation is common. This course does not assert a statewide percentage of self-represented plaintiffs; no supporting statistical breakdown has been established. Plaintiffs and defendants may both represent themselves. An individual can hire counsel even after initially proceeding alone.

## Rights, responsibilities and limits

Simplified proceedings do not eliminate legal obligations. A self-represented party remains responsible for applicable deadlines, correct party identification, pleadings, service, evidence, privacy, hearing attendance, compliance with orders and any review or collection procedures. The judicial officer is neutral, not the litigant's lawyer. Lack of legal training does not automatically excuse procedural mistakes. An attorney may help assess whether a claim, defense or remedy is legally available.

Self-representation is not permission to represent a friend, family member, organization or estate. Iowa Code §631.14 specifically authorizes certain nonlawyer representatives in small claims, including officers or employees of corporations and other entities, with additional provisions for rental property. Read the actual statute and seek legal advice if authority is unclear; do not assume this exception extends to regular civil proceedings. Instructors and classmates must not give individualized legal advice, act as another person's advocate or prepare actual legal instruments on their behalf. A disclaimer does not authorize the practice of law.

## What court personnel can and cannot do

Clerks and court navigators can provide procedural information, identify available resources and assist within their authorized roles. They cannot choose a legal strategy, tell a party whether to sue, interpret an agreement for a party or predict a verdict. Court navigators are available by appointment; consult the official program page for availability. An attorney can provide advice tailored to a person's circumstances. Ask about limited-scope representation if full representation is not needed or affordable; availability and scope must be agreed with the attorney.

## Practical self-assessment before choosing representation

Can I identify the actual parties and legal issue? Can I locate and understand the current rules? Can I reliably track deadlines, protect private information, organize admissible evidence, explain the claimed amount and attend hearings? Can I evaluate the time, costs, uncertainty and collectability of the claim? If an answer is uncertain, seek qualified legal counsel. Arbitration, disputed jurisdiction or service, counterclaims, appeals, bankruptcy and complex enforcement are particular reasons to consult counsel. Legal aid eligibility varies.

## Class activity | 45–60 minutes

10 minutes: define pro se and compare plaintiff and defendant responsibilities. 15 minutes: locate the official self-representation guidance and §631.14. 15 minutes: examine the fictional Morgan/PrairieLink case and identify what each party must establish and what legal questions remain. 10 minutes: classify the requests below as procedural information or individualized advice. Optional discussion: advantages and disadvantages of retaining counsel. Never ask students to disclose their real disputes.
